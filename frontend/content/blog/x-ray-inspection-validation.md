Plants usually install X-ray inspection for a reason: foil or metallized packaging, products a metal detector struggles with, or a customer who requires it. That makes the X-ray system one of the most important foreign material controls in the building, and auditors expect proof that it works as well as you say it does.

Here's how X-ray validation works, and how it differs from validating a metal detector.

## What X-ray validation proves

Validation shows that your X-ray system reliably **detects and rejects** the contaminants your food safety plan names, at the sizes your plan specifies, **in your actual products and packaging**, at your normal line speed. That last part matters. A system that finds a test piece on an empty belt may miss the same piece inside a dense or uneven product.

## How X-ray is different from a metal detector

X-ray systems find contaminants by **density**, not by whether they're metal. That changes how you validate them:

- **Position matters differently.** A metal detector is least sensitive in the center of the aperture. With X-ray, detection is hardest where the product is **densest or most uneven**, such as thick sections, overlapping pieces, or the edges of a tray.
- **Product variation matters.** Products with mixed ingredients or varying fill levels can hide contaminants or trigger false rejects.
- **Orientation matters.** Thin, flat contaminants can be hard to see edge-on.
- **The product program matters.** Most systems "learn" each product. Validation should use the same program you run in production.

## Choosing test pieces

Most food plants rely on X-ray mainly to find **metal**, so validation usually uses certified **ferrous, non-ferrous, and stainless steel** test pieces, with stainless steel often detected well compared with a metal detector.

If your food safety plan names other hazards that X-ray can detect, such as **glass, stone, or calcified bone**, validate those too, using certified test standards of the sizes your plan specifies. These standards are made for X-ray testing, often embedded in a carrier so they can be placed in or under the product.

## What a thorough validation covers

- **Each contaminant type and size** your plan specifies
- **Multiple positions in the product**, including the hardest-to-inspect areas
- **Repeat passes**, so detection is consistent, not lucky
- **Every product and package type** run on the line, or a justified worst case
- **Reject confirmation**: contaminated product is actually removed into a secure bin
- **Fail-safes**: reject confirmation, bin full, air pressure, and system fault alarms
- **As-found and as-left settings**, and what was changed if something failed

## What validation doesn't cover

X-ray validation is about **food safety performance**. It's separate from **radiation safety**, such as state registration of the equipment, radiation surveys, and checks of the safety interlocks that protect your employees. Keep those on their own schedule, through your equipment supplier or a radiation safety provider.

## How often?

At least **once a year**, and again whenever the system is installed, moved, or serviced, when you add a new product or packaging format, or when a customer or certification body requires it. Your operators should also check the system with test pieces during production, just as they would a metal detector.
