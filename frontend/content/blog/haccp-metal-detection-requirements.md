"What are the HACCP requirements for metal detection?" It's one of the most common questions we hear from Utah food plants, and the answer surprises people: there's no rule that says every plant must have a metal detector. What the rules require is that you **evaluate metal as a hazard** and, if it's a real risk, **control it and prove the control works**.

Here's how that plays out under HACCP and FDA's rules, when a metal detector becomes a critical control point, and what your plan and records need to show.

## Does Utah have its own metal detection requirements?

No. Utah doesn't have a separate state law for metal detection in food plants. Utah food manufacturers follow the federal rules that apply to them:

- **FDA-regulated plants** follow the **Preventive Controls for Human Food rule (21 CFR Part 117)** under the Food Safety Modernization Act.
- **Meat and poultry plants** follow **USDA FSIS HACCP regulations (9 CFR Part 417)**.
- **Juice and seafood processors** have their own FDA HACCP rules.

On top of the regulations, most plants also answer to **GFSI schemes** like SQF and BRCGS, and to their **customers' requirements**, which often set specific test piece sizes and test frequencies.

## Step 1: Is metal a hazard in your product?

Every HACCP plan and food safety plan starts with a **hazard analysis**. For physical hazards, ask:

- Could metal get into the product from **raw materials**, such as wire, staples, or fragments from harvesting equipment?
- Could it come from **your process**: worn blades, screens, mixers, slicers, grinders, broken parts, or maintenance work?
- How likely is it, and how severe would it be? FDA guidance treats hard or sharp foreign objects from **7 to 25 mm** as a potential injury hazard.

If metal is reasonably likely to occur and could cause harm, you need a control for it. For most plants, that control is a **metal detector** or an **X-ray inspection system**.

## Step 2: Is the metal detector a critical control point?

A **critical control point (CCP)** is a step where control is essential to prevent, eliminate, or reduce a hazard to an acceptable level. If your metal detector is the step that removes metal from your product before it ships, it's usually a CCP.

Under FDA's Preventive Controls rule, the same idea is called a **process preventive control**. The name differs, but the expectations are much the same.

Not every metal detector is a CCP. A detector early in the line that protects equipment, with a final detector at packaging, may be a control point rather than a CCP. Your hazard analysis should say which is which, and why.

## Step 3: What your plan needs for a metal detector CCP

| Element | What it means for a metal detector |
|---|---|
| **Critical limit** | The ferrous, non-ferrous, and stainless steel test piece sizes the detector must find and reject, in your product |
| **Monitoring** | Operators check the detector with certified test pieces at start-up, at set intervals during production, at changeovers, and at shutdown |
| **Corrective action** | What happens when a check fails: stop the line, hold all product since the last good check, re-inspect it, and fix the detector |
| **Verification** | Supervisors review monitoring records, test pieces are certified and in good condition, and the detector is validated |
| **Validation** | Proof the detector meets the critical limit in your real products, at your line speed, with a reject system and fail-safes that work |
| **Records** | Monitoring logs, corrective actions, verification reviews, and validation certificates, kept and ready for an auditor |

## What auditors look for

When an auditor reviews your metal detector CCP, expect questions like:

- What test piece sizes do you use, and **why those sizes**?
- Show me **today's monitoring checks**, and what you did the last time a check failed.
- Are your test pieces **certified**, and when were they last inspected?
- When was the detector last **validated**, and does the record show every product, every test piece, and the fail-safes?
- Does contaminated product actually end up in a **locked reject bin**?

Most findings aren't because the detector failed. They're because the paperwork can't show it works.

## What about X-ray systems?

Everything above applies to X-ray inspection too. If your X-ray system is the step that removes metal, glass, stone, or bone, it's usually a CCP with the same critical limits, monitoring, corrective actions, verification, and validation. ## The bottom line

HACCP doesn't require a metal detector by name. It requires you to control metal if it's a hazard, and to prove that control works. For most plants, that means a metal detector or X-ray system set up as a critical control point, with clear critical limits, routine monitoring, and regular validation.
