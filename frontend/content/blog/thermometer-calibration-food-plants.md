Every critical limit in your food safety plan that involves temperature depends on a thermometer: cook temperatures, cooling, cold storage, hot holding. If the thermometer reads two degrees low, your records say product is safe when it might not be.

That's why auditors look closely at how you calibrate your thermometers. Here's what thermometer calibration really means, how it's done, and how often food plants should do it.

## What thermometer calibration means

**Calibration** is comparing a thermometer with a reference standard you can trust and recording how far off it reads. That difference is the **error**. If the error is within your tolerance, the thermometer passes. If it isn't, you adjust the thermometer (if it can be adjusted) or take it out of service.

Calibration doesn't require adjusting anything. A thermometer that reads 0.2 °C high, within a ±1.0 °C tolerance, is calibrated and fine to use, as long as you've documented it.

## Two levels of checks

Most food plants need both of these.

### 1. Routine checks by your own team

The **ice point check** is the classic quick check for probe thermometers:

1. Fill a container with crushed ice and add just enough cold water to fill the gaps.
2. Stir and let it sit for a few minutes.
3. Put the probe in the center of the ice, not touching the sides or bottom, and wait for the reading to settle.
4. It should read 0 °C (32 °F), within the thermometer's tolerance.

These checks are quick and catch problems between calibrations. Many plants do them daily, weekly, or at the start of each shift for thermometers used on critical control points.

A note for Utah: don't use boiling water as a 100 °C (212 °F) check without correcting for altitude. Along the Wasatch Front, water boils several degrees lower, around 94 to 95 °C (201 to 203 °F) depending on elevation and weather.

### 2. Periodic calibration against a traceable reference

An ice point check only proves one temperature. A full calibration compares each thermometer against a **reference thermometer that is itself calibrated and NIST-traceable**, at the temperatures where you actually use it, such as your cook or hot-holding temperature. It also produces a certificate with the readings, the error, the tolerance, and the reference used. That's what auditors expect to see in your files.

## What needs calibrating

- **Handheld and removable probes** used to check cook, cooling, and receiving temperatures
- **Fixed gauges and sensors** on kettles, ovens, fryers, cookers, and pasteurizers
- **Fridge, freezer, and walk-in thermometers**
- **Data loggers and recorders** used for monitoring records
- **Thermohygrometers** in dry storage and warehouses where humidity matters

## How often?

There isn't one interval for every thermometer. FDA's preventive controls rule expects monitoring and verification instruments to be calibrated, or checked for accuracy, as often as needed, with records. GFSI schemes like SQF and BRCGS expect a documented calibration program with defined frequencies.

A common, defensible approach:

- **Routine checks** (ice point or a comparison check) daily to weekly for thermometers on critical control points
- **Full calibration against a traceable reference** at least once a year, and more often for heavily used or critical instruments
- **Right away** after a thermometer is dropped, damaged, repaired, or fails a routine check

As with everything in your plan, write down your frequencies and why you chose them.

## What a good calibration certificate includes

- The thermometer's make, model, serial number, and location
- The reference standard used, its serial number, and its calibration date and due date
- The temperatures checked, the reference reading, the thermometer reading, and the error at each point
- The tolerance and a pass or fail for each point
- As-found and as-left readings if anything was adjusted
- The date, who did it, and when it's due next
