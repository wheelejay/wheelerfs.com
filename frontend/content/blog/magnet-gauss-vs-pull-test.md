Rare earth magnets in grates, drawers, plates, and tubes are a first line of defense against ferrous metal in food and supplement plants. But magnets weaken over time, and you can't tell by looking. So how do you prove yours still work?

The two common methods are the **gauss test** and the **pull test**. They measure different things, and knowing the difference helps you choose, and explain your choice to an auditor.

## The gauss test

A **gaussmeter** measures the strength of the magnetic field at the magnet's surface, in gauss.

**Strengths:**
- Quick, and gives a number that's easy to trend
- Widely recognized, and sometimes requested by auditors or customers

**Limits:**
- The reading depends heavily on **where and how the probe is placed**. A small change in position can change the result, so readings can vary between operators.
- Surface field strength doesn't directly tell you **how well the magnet holds metal** in a flowing product stream.
- The gaussmeter itself needs regular calibration.

## The pull test

A **pull test** measures the force needed to pull a standard steel test piece, often a ball or a small block, away from the magnet's surface.

**Strengths:**
- Directly reflects **holding power**, which is what catches and keeps metal
- Simple, repeatable, and easy to compare over time

**Limits:**
- Results depend on the **test piece** used. Different manufacturers' test pieces of the same nominal size can give quite different readings, so always use the same test piece and method.
- The pull tester needs to be in calibration.

## Which one should you use?

Many plants use **both**: pull testing for holding power, with gauss readings for trending, plus regular **visual inspection** of every magnet. Whatever you choose:

- **Use the same method, points, and test piece every time**, so results are comparable
- **Record a baseline** when the magnet is new, or at its first test
- **Trend the results** to spot a magnet that's weakening

## Setting acceptance limits

There's no single industry-wide number for how strong a rare earth magnet must be. Plants commonly set limits based on:

- The **manufacturer's specification** for the magnet
- A **maximum allowed drop** from the magnet's original strength
- **Customer requirements**

Write your limit, and your reasoning, into your foreign material control program. When a magnet falls below it, replace it, and review whether product that passed it while it was weak needs attention.

## Don't forget inspection and cleaning

Strength testing is only part of magnet control. Magnets also need:

- **Regular cleaning**, so captured metal doesn't build up and wash back into product
- **Visual inspection** for cracked tubes, damaged welds, and worn housings
- **Records of what's captured**, which can point to worn equipment upstream
