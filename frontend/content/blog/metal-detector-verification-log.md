Every metal detector on a critical control point needs a record that it was checked, and that it worked. That record is your **verification log**, and it's one of the first things an auditor asks to see.

We've put together a free, printable log you can use on your lines, along with how to run the checks properly.

Page 1 is the log. Page 2 is a one-page guide for operators and QA. Print as many copies as you need.

## What's on the log

- **Detector details**: site, line or detector ID, make, model, and serial number
- **Test pieces**: ferrous, non-ferrous, and stainless steel sizes and certificate numbers
- **Check frequency** from your food safety plan
- **For every check**: date, time, type of check, product or program, whether each test piece was detected and rejected, reject bin locked, fail-safe alarm working, pass or fail, corrective action reference, operator initials, and QA or supervisor sign-off
- **What to do if a check fails**, printed right on the sheet

## How to run a metal detector verification check

### Before you start

- Use the **certified test pieces** named in your food safety plan, at the sizes on the log.
- **Inspect each test piece.** Replace any that are cracked, worn, or unreadable; a damaged piece may not match its labeled size.
- Confirm the detector is running the **correct product program**.
- Sign the test pieces out of secure storage, and return them when you're done.

### Running the check

1. **Run each test piece with product**, in or on a pack, not on an empty belt, so product effect is included.
2. Place it where detection is hardest: the **center of the aperture**. Many plants also test the **leading and trailing edges** of the product.
3. Run **ferrous, then non-ferrous, then stainless steel**, one at a time, at normal line speed.
4. Each one must be **detected and rejected**. Watch the reject device fire and confirm the pack lands in the reject bin.
5. Check that the **reject bin is locked** and that the reject confirmation and alarms work.
6. **Retrieve the test pieces** and count them back. Never leave a test piece in product.
7. **Record the result** on the log and initial it.

### When to check

- At **start-up**, before any product is released
- **During production**, at the interval in your plan. Many plants check every one to two hours.
- At every **product or program changeover**
- **After maintenance**, adjustment, or any stoppage
- At the **end of the shift or run**, so the last product made is covered

The interval decides how much product you have to hold when a check fails. The longer the gap, the more product is at risk.

## If a check fails

1. **Stop the line** and tell QA or your supervisor right away.
2. **Hold all product** made since the last successful check.
3. **Find and fix the cause.** Don't just lower the sensitivity.
4. **Repeat the full check** until every test piece passes.
5. **Re-inspect the held product** through a working detector.
6. **Record the corrective action** and what happened to the held product.

For more detail, see [what to do when your metal detector fails a check](/blog/metal-detector-failed-check).

## Verification isn't validation

The log shows your detector is working **right now**. **Validation** proves it finds and rejects your specified test pieces in every product you run, with every fail-safe tested, and auditors expect it at least once a year. QA should also review and sign the verification logs regularly.
