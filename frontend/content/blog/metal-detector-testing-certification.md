"Can you certify our metal detector?" It's a fair question. Customers and auditors ask for proof, and a certificate feels like the answer. But metal detector testing and certification isn't governed by one official standard or licensing body, so what's on that certificate matters a lot more than the word "certified."

Here's what the industry actually expects, the different kinds of checks, and what a certificate worth having should show.

## Is there an official metal detector standard?

Not one single rule that sets test piece sizes for every plant. Instead, expectations come from several places:

- **FDA.** Under the Food Safety Modernization Act, metal is a hazard you evaluate in your food safety plan. FDA guidance treats hard or sharp foreign objects from 7 to 25 mm as a potential injury hazard, so your detection capability should be well below that.
- **USDA FSIS**, for meat and poultry plants, through your HACCP plan.
- **GFSI schemes** such as SQF and BRCGS, which expect detection equipment to be in place where needed, tested at defined frequencies with documented results, and backed by fail-safes and corrective actions.
- **Your customers.** Many retailers and brand owners publish their own requirements, including test piece sizes, test frequencies, and reject system features. When they apply, they're usually the strictest.

In other words, the "standard" for your metal detector is the one written in your own food safety plan, built on these sources.

## The three kinds of metal detector checks

| Check | Who does it | How often | What it proves |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Routine checks (verification)** | Your operators | Start-up, during production, changeovers, shutdown | The detector is working right now |
| **Validation** | A qualified person, often a third party | At least yearly, and after changes | The detector finds and rejects the specified test pieces in your real products |
| **Service and repair** | The manufacturer or a service technician | As needed | The equipment is mechanically and electrically sound |

All three matter. A "metal detector check" usually means the first one, the quick operator test with test pieces. A "certification" should mean the second.

## What meaningful testing includes

Whoever tests your detector, a meaningful test should:

- Use **certified ferrous, non-ferrous, and stainless steel test pieces** at the sizes your plan specifies
- Test **in your real products**, including the hardest ones to inspect
- Pass test pieces at the **leading edge, center, and trailing edge** of the product, through the center of the aperture
- Repeat each test, for example **three passes in a row**, so one lucky detection doesn't count
- Confirm that **contaminated product is actually rejected** into a secure bin
- Check every **fail-safe**: reject confirmation, bin full, air pressure, and detector fault alarms
- Record **as-found and as-left settings**, and what was adjusted if something failed

## What your certificate should show

A certificate is only as good as the evidence on it. Look for:

- Detector make, model, serial number, and line
- Products tested and settings used
- Test piece types, sizes, and certificate numbers
- Results for every test piece in every position
- Reject and fail-safe results
- As-found and as-left status
- The date, the technician, and a next-due date
- A way to **confirm it's genuine**

A certificate that just says "tested and certified" without the details is hard to defend in an audit.
