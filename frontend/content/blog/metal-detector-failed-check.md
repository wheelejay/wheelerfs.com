An operator runs the stainless steel test piece through the metal detector, and nothing happens. The detector didn't see it, or it saw it and the reject didn't fire. What now?

A failed check is exactly what your monitoring is designed to catch. Handled well, it's a non-event in an audit. Handled poorly, it can mean a finding, or product in the field that shouldn't be. Here's what to do.

## Step 1: Stop and confirm

- **Stop the line**, or divert product so nothing more is packed past the detector.
- **Tell QA or your supervisor** right away.
- **Confirm the failure.** Check that the right test piece was used, it isn't damaged, the detector is on the correct product program, and the test was run correctly. Then repeat the check. If it still fails, treat it as a real failure.

## Step 2: Hold the product

Everything made **since the last successful check** is now suspect, because you can't prove the detector was working when it was produced. Put it on hold, clearly labeled and segregated. This is why your check interval matters: the longer the gap between checks, the more product you have to hold.

## Step 3: Find the cause

Common reasons a metal detector fails a check:

- **Wrong product program** selected after a changeover
- **Sensitivity changed**, by accident or because someone lowered it to stop false rejects
- **Product effect**: a new formulation, temperature, or package size the detector was never set up for
- **Reject system problems**: low air pressure, a sticky valve, a mistimed reject, a full or jammed reject bin
- **Belt speed or encoder problems** that throw off reject timing
- **Electrical interference** from nearby equipment, or damage to the detector head
- **A damaged or wrong-size test piece**

Fix the cause, not the symptom. Turning down the sensitivity until the test piece passes isn't a fix.

## Step 4: Fix, then re-verify

After the repair or adjustment, **run the full check again**: every test piece, every position you normally test, with product, and confirm each one is detected **and** rejected into the locked bin. If the problem needed a significant repair or setting change, consider a **revalidation** before you rely on the detector again.

## Step 5: Re-inspect the held product

Run the held product back through a **working, verified detector**, or another approved control. Product that's rejected on re-inspection needs investigating: what was found, and where did it come from? Document what happened to every held lot.

## Step 6: Record everything

Your corrective action record should show:

- When the failure was found, and by whom
- Which product was held, and how much
- The cause, and what was done to fix it
- The successful re-check after the fix
- The result of re-inspecting the held product, and its final disposition
- Any steps to stop it happening again, such as training, a new product program, or a shorter check interval

An auditor reading that record should see a plant that caught a problem and handled it, not one that hid it.

## Preventing the next one

- Make **product program selection** part of every changeover checklist
- **Password-protect** sensitivity settings
- Revalidate when you **change products, packaging, or line speed**
- Keep **test pieces** inspected and replaced when damaged
- Validate every detector **at least once a year**
