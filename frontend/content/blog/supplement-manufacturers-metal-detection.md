Utah is home to a large share of the country's dietary supplement manufacturers, from contract manufacturers to brand owners. Tablets, capsules, powders, and gummies all run through equipment with plenty of metal parts, and metal contamination is a real risk.

Here's what the supplement GMP rule says about metal, where it comes from, and how supplement makers prove their metal controls work.

## What 21 CFR 111 says about metal

Supplement manufacturing is covered by FDA's **dietary supplement current good manufacturing practice rule, 21 CFR Part 111**. Section **111.365** requires manufacturers to take precautions against contamination during production. One of those precautions is protecting against **metal or other foreign material** in components and finished supplements, and the rule gives examples: **filters or strainers, traps, magnets, or electronic metal detectors**.

The rule doesn't name test piece sizes or a testing frequency. Those are up to you, based on your products and process, written into your procedures, and backed by records. Auditors and customers will expect to see:

- Which metal controls you use, and where in the process
- The test piece sizes or limits each control must meet
- Routine checks, with records
- What you do when a check fails
- Proof the control works, such as a validation

Many supplement makers are also audited to **NSF/ANSI 173 GMP, SQF, or customer programs**, which add their own expectations for foreign material control.

## Where metal gets into supplements

- **Tablet presses**: punches, dies, and tooling wear and can chip
- **Encapsulation machines**: worn change parts and dosing equipment
- **Mixers and blenders**: blades, paddles, and ribbons
- **Mills, sifters, and screens**: broken screens and worn mill parts
- **Raw materials**: metal fragments from suppliers' processing
- **Maintenance**: tools, wire, nuts, bolts, and shims left behind

## The usual metal controls

- **Magnets** in powder lines, chutes, and hoppers to catch ferrous fines
- **Screens and sifters** to catch larger fragments
- **Metal detectors** on packaging lines, and inspection after tablet presses or encapsulators
- **X-ray inspection** when products are in foil or blister packs, or a customer requires it

Each one needs routine checks, records, and proof that it works.

## Validation: proving the controls work

A validation shows that your metal detector or X-ray system finds and rejects the specified ferrous, non-ferrous, and stainless steel test pieces **in your actual product**: the densest tablet, the fullest bottle, the product that's hardest to inspect. It also confirms the reject system removes contaminated product and the fail-safes work. For magnets, validation means inspection and strength testing.

## The bottom line

21 CFR 111 expects you to protect against metal, and it names metal detectors and magnets as ways to do it. It doesn't tell you the details. Set your limits, check them routinely, keep the records, and validate your controls so you can prove they work.
