If your plant is certified to **SQF** or **BRCGS**, your metal detectors and X-ray systems will be examined at every audit. Both schemes treat foreign material detection as a core control, and both expect you to prove it works, not just that it's installed.

Here's what each scheme expects in practice, and the common findings to avoid. Clause numbers change between editions, so always check the edition you're certified to.

## SQF

In the **SQF Food Safety Code for Manufacturing**, detection of foreign objects is covered in the good manufacturing practices module (Module 11 in Edition 9), alongside your food safety plan in the system elements. In practice, auditors expect:

- A **risk-based decision** on where detection equipment is needed, documented in your food safety plan
- Detection equipment that is **checked at a defined frequency**, with records
- Defined **test piece sizes**, and certified test pieces
- **Corrective actions** when a check fails, including what happens to product since the last good check
- **Verification** that monitoring is happening, and **validation** that the control works

SQF also requires a designated **SQF Practitioner** to maintain the food safety plan, and auditors often ask that person to explain how the metal detector CCP was set up and validated.

## BRCGS

The **BRCGS Global Standard for Food Safety** covers foreign body detection and removal equipment in **Section 4.10** (Issue 9), with metal detectors and X-ray equipment in **4.10.3**. BRCGS is generally more specific than SQF. Expect auditors to look for:

- A **documented assessment** of whether detection equipment is needed, and which type
- Test pieces of **defined size and material**, run in or with product
- **Testing at a defined frequency**, with records and trained operators
- **Automatic reject systems** where possible, with contaminated product going to a **locked** unit or container
- **Fail-safe systems** that alert or stop the line if the detector or reject system fails
- **Clear corrective actions**, including what happens to product since the last good test

## What both schemes have in common

1. **Justify your test piece sizes.** "That's what we've always used" isn't a reason.
2. **Test the hardest case**: in product, through the center of the aperture.
3. **Prove rejection**, not just detection.
4. **Test your fail-safes**, and record it.
5. **Keep complete records**: verification logs, corrective actions, and validation certificates.
6. **Validate at least once a year**, and after changes.

## The most common findings

- Missing or incomplete **verification records**
- **Test pieces** that are damaged, uncertified, or not the sizes in the plan
- Checks run **without product**, or only at the most sensitive position
- **Reject bins** that aren't locked, or can't be shown to receive rejected product
- **Fail-safes** that were never tested
- **Corrective actions** that don't cover product made since the last good check
- No **validation**, or one that's out of date or incomplete
