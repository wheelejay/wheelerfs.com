Food manufacturers choosing foreign material detection usually land on one of two technologies: metal detection or X-ray inspection. Both can protect your customers and satisfy auditors, but they work in completely different ways, and the right choice depends on your products, your packaging, and what your customers expect.

Here's a practical comparison to help you decide.

## How each one works

**Metal detectors** create an electromagnetic field. When a conductive or magnetic object passes through, it disturbs the field and the detector triggers a reject. They detect metal, and only metal.

**X-ray systems** pass low-energy X-rays through the product and measure how much is absorbed. Dense objects absorb more and show up as dark spots in the image. That means X-ray finds contaminants by **density**, not by whether they're metal.

## What each one can detect

**Metal detectors** are very good at:

- Ferrous metal (iron and steel), usually the easiest to detect
- Non-ferrous metal (brass, aluminum, copper)
- Stainless steel, though it's typically the hardest of the three

**X-ray systems** can detect:

- Metal, including stainless steel
- Glass
- Stone and dense minerals
- Calcified bone
- Some dense plastics and rubber

X-ray systems can also check things a metal detector can't, such as missing items, fill levels, and damaged or broken product, depending on the model.

What **neither** detects well: low-density contaminants like wood, paper, soft plastics, and insects. Those still depend on good GMPs and upstream controls.

## Where metal detectors struggle

- **Metallized film and foil packaging.** Most standard detectors can't inspect products already in foil or metallized wrappers. (Some specialized detectors find ferrous metal in foil, but sensitivity is limited.)
- **Product effect.** Wet, salty, and conductive products like meats, cheeses, sauces, and brined foods create their own signal, which can mask small pieces of metal and force lower sensitivity.
- **Non-metal hazards.** If glass, stone, or bone is a real hazard in your process, a metal detector won't help.

## Where X-ray struggles

- **Cost.** X-ray systems usually cost significantly more to buy and maintain than metal detectors.
- **Thin or flat contaminants.** A thin piece of metal or glass can be hard to see when it passes edge-on.
- **Product density variation.** Products with uneven density, overlapping pieces, or mixed ingredients can hide contaminants or cause false rejects.
- **Regulatory paperwork.** X-ray equipment may need to be registered with your state radiation control program, plus training and periodic radiation surveys. Check Utah's current requirements before you buy.

## How to choose

A metal detector is often the right choice when:

- Metal is your main foreign material hazard
- Your product isn't in foil or metallized packaging
- Product effect is manageable
- Budget matters

X-ray is often the better choice when:

- Your product is in foil or metallized packaging
- Glass, stone, or bone is a real hazard (for example, glass jars or meat and poultry products)
- Product effect makes metal detection unreliable
- A customer or retailer specifically requires X-ray inspection

Many plants use both: metal detectors on most lines, and X-ray where packaging or hazards demand it.

## Either way, validation is required

Whichever technology you choose, your food safety plan needs proof that it works in your products, on your line. That means:

- Certified test pieces sized to your hazard analysis
- Testing at the leading edge, center, and trailing edge of the product
- Confirming that contaminated product is actually rejected
- Checking every fail-safe
- An audit-ready report for each unit

Metal detectors are reliable and affordable for metal hazards in most products. X-ray costs more but handles foil packaging and non-metal hazards that metal detectors can't see. Choose based on your actual hazards, packaging, and customer requirements, then validate whatever you install.
