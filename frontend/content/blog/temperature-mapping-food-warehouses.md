Most food warehouses, coolers, and freezers have a temperature sensor on the wall and a log that says everything is fine. But that sensor only tells you the temperature at one spot. The top rack near the door, the back corner behind a pallet, and the area next to the evaporator can all be very different.

**Temperature mapping** shows you what's really happening across the whole space.

## What is temperature mapping?

Temperature mapping is a study that places multiple calibrated data loggers throughout a storage area and records temperatures at regular intervals over a set period. The results show:

- **Hot and cold spots** where product could fall out of its required range
- **How evenly** temperature is distributed through the space
- **How the space responds** to door openings, defrost cycles, loading, and outside weather
- **The best locations** for your permanent monitoring sensors

## When do food companies need it?

Food regulations rarely say "you must map your warehouse" in so many words. But temperature mapping is often the most practical way to meet requirements and expectations that do apply:

- **GFSI audits (SQF, BRCGS).** Auditors expect temperature-controlled storage to be controlled and monitored. Mapping shows that your monitoring sensor is in a location that represents the whole space, not just the most convenient spot on the wall.
- **Customer requirements.** Many retailers and brand owners expect suppliers to show temperature control throughout storage and distribution.
- **Pharmaceutical and supplement storage.** If you store or handle nutraceutical, supplement, or pharmaceutical products, mapping is a common expectation for good storage and distribution practices.

Mapping is especially valuable when:

- A **new** cooler, freezer, or warehouse is built
- You **modify** a space, such as new racking, layout changes, or new refrigeration equipment
- You have **temperature excursions** or complaints you can't explain
- The **seasons** change significantly, since summer and winter can produce very different results

## How a mapping study works

Every space is different, but a typical study follows these steps.

### 1. Plan

Review the size and layout of the space, racking, doors, refrigeration units, and how product is stored. Decide how many loggers are needed and where they'll go, how long the study will run, and whether to map loaded, empty, or both.

### 2. Place the loggers

Calibrated data loggers are placed in a three-dimensional grid: high, middle, and low levels, front to back and side to side, with extra loggers near likely problem areas such as doors, docks, evaporators, and exterior walls.

### 3. Record

The loggers record temperatures at regular intervals, often over several days, so the data captures normal operations such as door openings, defrost cycles, and shift changes, not just a quiet afternoon.

### 4. Analyze and report

The data is analyzed to find the warmest and coldest locations, the overall temperature spread, and whether any location fell outside your required range. The final report includes:

- Logger locations and calibration information
- Temperature results for every location
- Identified hot and cold spots
- Recommendations for monitoring sensor placement and any corrective actions

## Acting on the results

A mapping study is only useful if you act on it. Common follow-ups include:

- **Moving your monitoring sensor** to the warmest (or coldest) representative location
- **Restricting storage** in problem areas, such as directly in front of doors or evaporators
- **Adjusting airflow** or racking to even out temperatures
- **Remapping** after changes, or seasonally if your results vary with the weather
