Your walk-in cooler's display says 36 °F. Your freezer log says −5 °F. But those numbers come from one sensor, usually mounted wherever was easiest to wire. Product on the top shelf by the door, or in the back corner behind a stack of pallets, may be living at a very different temperature.

**Temperature mapping** shows you the real picture. Here's how it works in coolers and freezers, and why it matters.

## Why coolers and freezers have warm spots

Cold rooms fight heat all day. Temperatures vary because of:

- **Doors.** Every opening lets warm air in, and the area just inside the door feels it first and most.
- **Defrost cycles.** Freezer evaporators defrost on a schedule, and nearby product can warm noticeably during each cycle.
- **Airflow.** Cold air follows the path of least resistance. Tightly stacked pallets, product against walls, and blocked evaporator fans create dead zones.
- **Loading.** Warm product coming in, especially in large batches, raises temperatures around it until it's pulled down.
- **Height.** Warm air rises, so top racks often run warmer than floor level.
- **The building.** Exterior walls, roof sections, and sun-facing sides can make one side of a room warmer than the other, especially in a Utah summer.

## What a mapping study shows

Data loggers are placed throughout the cooler or freezer, high, middle, and low, front to back, with extra loggers near doors and evaporators. They record at regular intervals during normal operations, usually for several days. The results show:

- **The warmest and coldest spots** in the room
- **How much the temperature varies** across the space, and over time
- **How the room responds** to door traffic, defrost cycles, and loading
- **Whether anywhere goes outside your limits**, and for how long
- **Where your monitoring sensor should be**, so it watches the riskiest spot instead of the most convenient one

## When to map a cooler or freezer

- **New or rebuilt rooms**, before you rely on them
- **After changes**: new racking, a new evaporator or compressor, a different layout, or a big change in how much product you store
- **When something seems off**: unexplained excursions, ice buildup, frost on product, or customer complaints about temperature
- **Seasonally**, if the room behaves differently in summer and winter
- **When an auditor or customer asks** for evidence that the whole room, not just one spot, stays in range

## What to do with the results

- **Move your monitoring sensor** to a location that represents the warmest (or coldest) product
- **Keep product out of problem areas**, such as directly in front of the door or in an evaporator's defrost zone
- **Improve airflow** by changing how product is stacked or leaving clearance around fans and walls
- **Fix equipment issues** the data points to, such as long defrost recovery or a door that's left open too long
- **Re-map** after you make changes, to show they worked

## Don't forget the thermometers

A mapping study tells you where the room runs warm or cold. Your day-to-day monitoring still depends on the thermometer or sensor on the wall. Make sure it's calibrated too. See our post on [thermometer calibration for food plants](/blog/thermometer-calibration-food-plants).
