Magnet Pull Testing: How to Verify Your Magnets Still Work

2026-11-04 · Jordan Wheeler

Magnets are one of the simplest foreign material controls in a food plant. Grate magnets, plate magnets, and tube magnets sit in hoppers, chutes, and pipelines, quietly pulling ferrous metal out of your product before it reaches a metal detector or a customer.

But magnets don't last forever. They can lose strength gradually, and a weak magnet looks exactly like a strong one. If your food safety plan relies on magnets, you need proof that they still work.

Why magnets lose strength

Magnets can weaken over time because of:

  • Heat. High temperatures, including hot product and hot wash-down, can permanently reduce strength, especially for some rare-earth magnet grades.
  • Impact. Dropping magnets or knocking them during cleaning can crack the magnet inside the housing.
  • Age and wear. Housings wear thin, seals fail, and moisture can get in and damage the magnet.

The result is a magnet that still catches large pieces of metal but misses the small fragments it was installed to stop.

Three ways to check a magnet

A complete magnet check usually combines three methods.

1. Visual inspection

Look for:

  • Cracked, dented, or worn housings
  • Damaged welds or seals
  • Gaps or damage that could let product bypass the magnet
  • Correct installation, with no missing tubes or plates

A damaged housing can also become a source of foreign material itself.

2. Pull testing

A pull test measures how much force it takes to pull a standard steel test piece away from the magnet's surface. Because it reflects how strongly the magnet actually holds metal, it's a practical measure of capture performance.

Pull tests are taken at defined points on each magnet and compared with the manufacturer's original rating or with the results from the magnet's first test. A significant drop over time is a sign the magnet needs replacing.

3. Gauss readings

A gaussmeter measures the strength of the magnetic field at the surface. Gauss readings are useful for trending, but they depend heavily on probe position, so they should be taken at the same points each time. On their own, they don't always reflect how well a magnet holds metal, which is why many programs use them alongside pull testing.

What to do with the results

Build a simple record for each magnet:

  • A unique ID and location
  • Its original rating or baseline readings
  • Each test result, with the date
  • Your acceptance limit (for example, a maximum percentage drop from baseline, or the manufacturer's minimum)
  • Any corrective action taken

When a magnet falls below your limit, replace it, and review whether any product passed through while it was weak.

Don't forget cleaning and inspection

Strength testing is only part of the program. Magnets also need:

  • Regular cleaning so captured metal doesn't build up and wash back into product
  • Records of what's found. Recording what the magnet catches, and where, can point you to a worn part or failing piece of equipment upstream.

How often should magnets be tested?

Most plants have magnet strength tested at least once a year, with visual inspections and cleaning far more often, often every shift or every day depending on how much product flows through. Test again after any damage, relocation, or change in process. As with all foreign material controls, choose frequencies based on risk and write down your reasoning.
